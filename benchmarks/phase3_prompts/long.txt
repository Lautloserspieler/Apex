The following notes describe how a small regional rail company modernized its maintenance planning over three years. They are written as a plain narrative so that the text is long enough to exercise prompt processing, and they contain no special formatting or model-specific chat markup.

In the first year, the company kept its existing paper forms but began copying every inspection result into a shared spreadsheet at the end of each shift. The engineers quickly noticed that the same faults appeared on the same vehicles after long downhill sections of the northern line, where the brakes were used heavily. Wheel flats, worn brake pads and overheated bearings were recorded far more often on those trains than on the ones that served the flat coastal route. Until then, every vehicle had been inspected on the same fixed calendar, regardless of where it had been running.

The second year focused on collecting better data. Each train was fitted with inexpensive sensors that logged bearing temperature, vibration and the number of braking events per kilometer. The sensors were not perfect: some lost power in cold weather, others reported implausible values after being cleaned with high-pressure water. The team therefore wrote simple validation rules. A reading was discarded if it changed faster than physically possible, if it stayed exactly constant for several hours, or if it disagreed strongly with the sensors on the neighboring axle. Roughly four percent of all readings were removed this way, and the engineers documented every rule so that later analyses could be repeated.

With cleaner data, the planners compared three strategies. The first kept the fixed calendar. The second scheduled inspections after a fixed distance. The third combined distance with the measured braking load and bearing temperature, so that a train working the steep northern line would be inspected sooner than one on the coast. To keep the comparison fair, all three strategies were evaluated on the same historical period, with the same staff capacity and the same spare-part inventory. The planners were careful not to tune the third strategy on the very data used to judge it; they selected its thresholds on the first six months and measured its effect on the following twelve.

The results were mixed in an instructive way. The load-based strategy reduced unplanned breakdowns on the northern line by about a third, but it slightly increased the number of inspections that found nothing wrong on the coastal route, because the thresholds were initially too cautious. After the thresholds were adjusted, the total number of workshop hours stayed almost unchanged, while the number of trains withdrawn from service at short notice fell noticeably. Passengers mainly noticed that cancellations during the winter months became rarer.

In the third year, the company addressed the human side of the change. Experienced mechanics were skeptical of recommendations that came from a model they could not inspect. The planners responded by showing, for every recommended inspection, the specific measurements that had triggered it, and by allowing mechanics to record when they disagreed. Those disagreements turned out to be valuable: in several cases they revealed sensors that had been mounted incorrectly, and in one case they exposed a bearing type that aged differently from the rest of the fleet.

Looking back, the engineers summarized a few lessons. Measure before changing anything, and keep the raw data. Write down every rule used to clean or discard measurements. Compare strategies under identical conditions and on data that was not used to tune them. Report results where the new approach performs worse, not only where it performs better. Finally, make every recommendation explainable to the people who have to act on it.
